## Chapter 311 — Strength With a Dosage Table

Luo Wen disliked medicines that lied about being medicines.

He disliked poisons that lied about being medicines more.

Axtaya had somehow managed to offend him twice.

The laboratory beneath the Quaint Teahouse smelled of bitterroot, dried citrus peel and the metallic sweetness of heated blood. Three narrow lamps burned over a stone workbench. Between them sat two tiny glass vials, one scraped nearly clean, the other holding less than a finger’s depth of cloudy amber liquid.

Yun Shizhen leaned over both with the expression she usually reserved for people who said poison was merely a matter of making something toxic enough.

Luo watched her rotate the second vial beneath the lamp.

“You’ve been staring at that for half an hour.”

“I’m waiting for it to apologize.”

“It won’t.”

“Then I’ll keep judging it.”

Luo returned to his notes.

Across the table, Yun’s mouth moved very slightly.

He had learned, over the last two years, that this counted as victory.

The Quaint Teahouse had changed many things about both of them. It had not made Yun expressive.

She set the vial down.

“Again.”

Luo sighed.

“You said that three times.”

“And you were wrong twice.”

“I was not wrong.”

“You called the first layer a stimulant.”

“It is a stimulant.”

“It is also bait.”

Luo looked up.

Yun tapped the notes with one fingernail.

“The body feels the first acceleration and assumes the problem is ordinary exertion. Then this second compound blunts the warning response.”

“Pain?”

“Pain. Vascular resistance. Some of the defensive constriction that should happen when the meridians are being pushed too hard.”

Luo frowned.

“So the user thinks the body is tolerating more than it is.”

“The body knows better. The user does not.”

He looked at the sample again.

The first victim had briefly produced far more power than a Peak Marquis should have been able to sustain. The second had described the same impossible surge as exhilarating.

Neither had advanced.

Neither had broken through.

They had simply forced existing cultivation through the body at a rate the body was not built to carry.

Luo rubbed one thumb against the side of his forefinger.

“Third layer.”

Yun nodded.

“That one is uglier.”

He smiled without humor.

“You said the second one was ugly.”

“I was being polite.”

The door opened.

Lu Weiran entered carrying a bundle of shipping papers, two folded ledgers and exactly no interest in the smell of the room.

“Breakfast.”

Luo blinked.

“You brought invoices.”

“I ate already.”

Yun looked at the papers.

“Useful breakfast.”

Luo pointed at her.

“Do not encourage him.”

Lu ignored both of them and laid the bundle on the dry end of the workbench.

“The same weight categories appear on three separate shipments.”

Luo’s attention sharpened.

“What categories?”

Lu unfolded one sheet.

“Body mass. Resting pulse. Previous injury status. Estimated circulation tolerance.”

Yun’s eyes moved from the paper to the vial.

“Medical screening.”

“Yes.”

Lu slid over a second sheet.

“Different ports. Different merchant houses. Same table structure.”

Luo read it once.

Then again.

The columns were too neat to be improvised street distribution. Whoever had created the original protocol had cared about variation between bodies.

Not compassion.

Not necessarily.

But measurement.

“Whoever built this,” Luo said, “didn’t begin as a poisoner.”

Yun leaned back against the bench.

“That depends on how generous you feel toward physicians.”

“I’m a physician.”

“I know.”

“You’re dating one.”

“I make questionable decisions.”

Lu turned a page with complete composure.

Luo stared at him.

“Do you ever leave when people are having a private conversation?”

“No.”

Yun nodded once.

“Reliable.”

Luo closed his eyes.

When he reopened them, Yun was already pointing at the third compound pattern.

“The real problem is here.”

He followed.

Axtaya did not merely accelerate circulation.

It forced the body to keep supplying that acceleration after safer reserves should have dropped away. The chemistry pushed deep stress responses into usable throughput and delayed the collapse signal long enough for the user to continue fighting.

Luo felt the back of his neck tighten.

“Future vitality.”

Yun’s expression flattened.

“Yes.”

The phrase was imprecise, but every physician understood the thing beneath it. The reserves a body spent over years without noticing. Recovery capacity. Organ resilience. The margin between a wound becoming survivable and becoming final.

The first victim had lost some of that margin.

Rhen had repaired torn meridians, bleeding and organ strain.

He had not restored what had already been consumed.

Luo looked toward the ceiling as if he could see through two floors and find Rhen somewhere above serving tea.

“Even he called it spent.”

Yun said, “Because it was.”

Lu folded his arms.

“Can we quantify it?”

“Not yet,” Luo said.

Yun added, “But someone else probably can.”

Lu’s gaze moved to her.

“The dosage tables.”

She nodded.

“Those aren’t just about keeping users alive. They’re about predicting how much a body can be made to surrender before it stops being useful.”

For several breaths nobody spoke.

Outside the laboratory, footsteps passed overhead. Someone laughed. A kettle lid rattled. The teahouse was open.

Life continued one floor above a table covered in documents explaining how to borrow from it.

Luo picked up the vial.

“We need to know whether this was designed for emergency medicine and corrupted afterward, or designed for combat from the start.”

Yun watched the amber liquid slide along the glass.

“Does the difference matter to the dead?”

“It matters to whoever still has the original formula.”

That answer pleased neither of them.

Lu opened one of the new ledgers.

“I have three foreign route fragments.”

Yun looked over.

“From where?”

“Still working on that.”

“Useful.”

“I try.”

Luo reached for the ledger.

Lu pulled it away.

“You’ll get blood on it.”

“It is a medical investigation.”

“It is my ledger.”

Yun, without looking up, said, “He loves those more than he loves people.”

Lu considered this.

“Some people.”

Luo laughed despite himself.

The laugh faded quickly.

On the table, Axtaya waited in its little glass vial.

Strength with a dosage table.

Power measured against a future the user could not see being shortened.

And somewhere beyond Wuyue, somebody had once decided that this was acceptable mathematics.

## Chapter 312 — Frost Where Rhen Did Not Mean It

Rhen froze the teapot by accident.

He noticed because Sera stopped talking.

That was usually a useful warning.

They were in the upstairs room after closing, the shutters open to a warm evening and the crooked sign downstairs creaking whenever the breeze found it. Sera sat cross-legged at the low table with three regional reports spread around her and one sock missing for reasons neither of them could explain.

Rhen had been listening to her complain about an irrigation dispute.

Then the teapot went white.

Not cold.

White.

A skin of frost raced from the base to the lid and stopped only when a thin crack sounded from the ceramic.

Sera looked at the pot.

Then at him.

Rhen looked at the pot too.

“That seems excessive.”

“You think?”

“I was making tea.”

“You murdered it.”

“The leaves are probably fine.”

“The pot is dead.”

Rhen touched one finger to the rim.

The frost withdrew immediately.

The crack remained.

Sera put down the report she had been pretending to read.

“How long?”

Rhen glanced at her.

“How long what?”

“How long have you been doing that when I’m not looking?”

“I don’t generally freeze pottery when you’re not looking.”

“Rhen.”

He sighed.

There were people in Wuyue who would rather fight a Sovereign than hear that tone from Sera.

Rhen understood them.

“A few weeks.”

Her eyes narrowed.

“Define few.”

“Four.”

“Four is not a few when the problem is spontaneous winter.”

“It is a small winter.”

She reached across the table and flicked him in the forehead.

Rhen let her.

That was marriage.

Sera shifted closer.

“Show me.”

He held out his hand.

She placed two fingers against his wrist.

Her expression changed immediately.

Not because there was pressure.

Rhen’s suppression was still absurdly good. To anyone else, his pulse would have felt quiet and ordinary.

Sera had lived inside the Garden long enough to know what quiet was supposed to feel like.

This quiet was crowded.

Her fingers stayed there.

“You’re full again.”

“Yes.”

“How full?”

Rhen considered lying.

He did not.

“More than after the Isgard finale.”

Sera’s gaze snapped up.

“That was two years ago.”

“I know.”

“And you’ve used qi constantly since then.”

“Healing. Garden maintenance. Training repairs. The occasional mountain.”

She stared at him.

“The occasional mountain?”

“It was in the way.”

“That is not the important part of that sentence.”

Rhen smiled faintly.

Sera did not.

He let the smile go.

“The reserve comes back too fast.”

“How fast?”

“Faster than I can comfortably disperse it without making the dispersal itself noticeable.”

That was the real problem.

Frozen Petals Garden had spent years refining more than the people living beneath it. Its passive cycle restored, purified and returned what Rhen spent with increasing efficiency.

For Sera, Qin, Tae, Huo and the others, that refinement had strengthened foundations.

For Rhen, whose baseline reserve was already ridiculous, efficiency had become accumulation.

Sera’s fingers pressed slightly harder against his wrist.

“Does it hurt?”

“No.”

“Uncomfortable?”

“Yes.”

“How much?”

“Like being too full after dinner.”

She waited.

Rhen added, “If dinner were inside my meridians.”

“That is a terrible explanation.”

“It is accurate.”

Sera pulled his hand closer and turned it palm-up.

“No new technique.”

Rhen blinked.

“What?”

“No experimenting because you’re bored.”

“I’m not bored.”

“You’re always bored when the problem is inside your own body.”

“That seems unfair.”

“You once tested a healing variation on yourself because you wanted to know whether broken ribs felt different when repaired from the inside.”

“They did.”

“Rhen.”

He looked appropriately chastened.

It was not convincing.

Sera knew him too well.

She moved around the table and settled partly against his side, still holding his wrist.

For a while neither spoke.

Outside, the town moved through an ordinary night. A cart rolled over stone. Someone called for a child to come home. A dog barked twice and stopped.

Sera rested her cheek against his shoulder.

“Can you fix it?”

“Yes.”

He answered too quickly.

She lifted her head.

“Can you?”

Rhen looked out the window.

“I think so.”

“That is a different answer.”

“It needs less space.”

She followed his gaze.

“The qi.”

“Yes.”

“You mean condense it.”

“Eventually.”

The word sat between them.

Not tonight.

Not tomorrow.

But eventually.

Sera took that in without making it larger than it was.

They had survived too many catastrophes to panic at every strange symptom.

They had also survived because they did not pretend strange symptoms were normal.

“When it stops being pottery,” she said, “you tell me.”

Rhen glanced down at her.

“What does that mean?”

“When you start freezing walls. People. Tae’s ridiculous metal weights.”

“Tae would be upset.”

“That is why I listed them after people.”

Rhen laughed quietly.

Sera kissed the corner of his mouth.

Then she leaned away just enough to look at him.

“Promise.”

Rhen’s expression softened.

“I promise.”

She settled back against him.

After a moment she said, “You owe me a teapot.”

“I’ll buy one tomorrow.”

“Expensive one.”

“You break expensive ones too.”

“That was Huo.”

“Huo broke the table.”

“Then maybe it was Qin.”

“Qin is blind, not clumsy.”

Sera considered.

“Tae?”

“Tae would deny it while holding the pieces.”

“That sounds right.”

Rhen reached for the cracked pot.

Sera slapped his hand away.

“No.”

“I can still use it.”

“You froze it to death.”

“It’s ceramic.”

“It had a good life.”

Rhen looked down at her.

Sera’s eyes were closed.

He let the pot go.

A tiny crystal of frost formed on the wooden table beside his thumb.

He noticed it this time.

It vanished before Sera opened her eyes.

## Chapter 313 — Leaving Is Still a Choice

Xie Wuchen was halfway through stealing a pear when Sera found him.

He was not good at theft.

This was mostly because he was standing on the Quaint Teahouse roof in broad daylight, wearing the same pale travel coat Shen Rui had already described to everyone, with the pear visible in his hand.

Xie looked at Sera as she stepped onto the roof.

“This is mine.”

“The pear?”

“Yes.”

“You took it from my kitchen.”

“I was given it.”

“By whom?”

Xie looked toward the courtyard below.

A twelve-year-old apprentice immediately ducked behind a medicine rack.

Sera followed his gaze.

“Traitor.”

From below came a muffled, “Sorry, Lady Sera.”

Xie took a bite.

“Good pear.”

Sera folded her arms.

“You’re very relaxed for a man who spent last week insisting he wanted nothing to do with us.”

“I still want nothing to do with you.”

“You are on my roof.”

“The view is good.”

“You brought Lu a ledger.”

“I found a ledger.”

“You crossed two counties to bring it here.”

“I was traveling this direction.”

“From the west.”

Xie chewed.

Sera waited.

Wind moved over the roof tiles.

The Unseen Gale had earned the title because people usually noticed the result of his movement before they noticed him. Tempest Hall’s last successor had built his entire martial identity around absence: no wasted sound, no unnecessary pressure, no path anyone could rely upon remaining occupied.

In person, Sera was discovering, that principle extended well beyond martial arts.

“You dislike institutions,” she said.

Xie’s expression did not change.

“I dislike being owned by them.”

“Not the same thing.”

“Often becomes the same thing.”

Sera sat on the roof ridge.

Xie looked at her.

She patted the tile beside her.

He stayed where he was.

“Fine,” she said. “Be dramatic.”

“I’m eating fruit.”

“You’re eating it mysteriously.”

That almost got him.

Almost.

His mouth moved at one corner.

Sera saw it.

She decided not to embarrass him by winning visibly.

“Rui said you saved six witnesses years ago.”

Xie’s gaze went to the distant hills.

“He said too much.”

“He said he was chasing them because his intelligence was compromised.”

“He was younger.”

“He said you beat him.”

“Also younger.”

“He is still upset about that part.”

“That seems healthy.”

Sera smiled.

Then the smile faded.

“Why did you leave?”

Xie finished chewing.

“After?”

“After you learned the witnesses were innocent. After Rui realized he’d been used. After you had every reason to stay long enough to make sure the people who created the bad intelligence couldn’t do it again.”

Xie’s eyes sharpened.

“You’ve been talking to him a lot.”

“He talks more when irritated.”

“That is unfortunate.”

“Why did you leave?”

Xie looked at the pear core.

“Because staying means somebody gives you a title.”

Sera waited.

“Then a responsibility,” he continued. “Then an office. Then one day somebody points at a person you’ve never met and says the banner requires you to kill them.”

“That happened to Tempest Hall?”

“It happened to everyone eventually.”

His answer was quiet.

Not bitter.

Worse than bitter.

Experienced.

Sera looked across the town below.

Quaint members moved in and out of the rear courtyard carrying medicine, tea, bedding and reports. Nobody marched in formation. Nobody saluted the roof.

One thousand people, all at least Marquis, and most of the town still thought the busiest thing they did was unload herbs.

“Do you think Tae obeys me because I own him?” she asked.

Xie glanced at her.

“No.”

“Huo?”

“No.”

“Qin?”

“Definitely not.”

“Good.”

She leaned back on both hands.

“They stay because they choose the structure. If they stop choosing it, I have a problem, not a deserter.”

Xie said nothing.

“That doesn’t mean every institution works that way,” Sera continued. “Most don’t. But you’ve made the opposite rule into a religion.”

His gaze narrowed.

“What opposite rule?”

“That depending on nobody keeps you morally clean.”

“I did not say that.”

“You behave it.”

The pear core stopped turning between his fingers.

Sera looked at him directly.

“You help somebody. Then you leave before they can ask for anything else.”

“Better than staying until they stop knowing where gratitude ends and obligation begins.”

“Sometimes.”

She stood.

“But leaving before anyone can depend on you is still a choice.”

That landed.

Xie’s face did not change much.

His eyes did.

Sera stepped past him toward the roof access.

Behind her, he said, “You were waiting to say that.”

“No.”

“You were.”

“I’m naturally wise.”

“That is not what Rui said.”

Sera stopped.

Slowly turned.

Xie had the good sense to take one step backward.

Wind gathered around his boots.

Sera pointed at him.

“Tell Rui I said he’s banned from tea for a week.”

“I’m not your courier.”

“You keep bringing ledgers here.”

“That is different.”

“See? Already useful.”

Xie vanished from the roof.

Not literally.

His body simply accelerated with such clean Wind-Qi that the eye lost him between one tile and the next.

A pale blur crossed the next building.

Then the next.

Sera leaned over the roof edge.

The apprentice who had given him the pear looked up.

“Did he join?”

“No.”

“Will he?”

Sera considered the empty roof.

“Ask me after he steals another pear.”

The apprentice nodded solemnly.

From the street beyond the teahouse came Xie’s voice, carried back by a perfectly ordinary gust.

“I heard that.”

Sera grinned.

Good.

He was still listening.
