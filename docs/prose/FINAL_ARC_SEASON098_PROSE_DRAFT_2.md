## Chapter 335 — Luo’s Private Notation

Luo Wen had terrible handwriting.

This was useful.

Years ago, Yun had told him so while stealing his case notes.

He had objected on the grounds that every physician understood them.

Yun had asked three physicians.

Two guessed.

One gave up.

Luo responded by inventing a second layer of notation specifically to annoy her.

That had lasted four days.

Then Yun improved it.

By the end of the month they had created a private shorthand no sane institution would approve.

It mixed dosage marks, pulse timing, toxin families, circulation landmarks and domestic jokes that had accidentally become symbols.

A dot beneath a number meant delayed systemic effect.

A diagonal slash meant “apparent symptom is not source.”

Two small circles meant paired circulation.

Three circles meant Luo had overcooked dinner badly enough that Yun refused to let him forget it.

Later, three circles came to mean “something presented as ordinary is structurally wrong.”

Medicine became memory.

Memory became language.

Now, in a rented room three months from home, Yun used that language to hide an invasion inside a physician’s notebook.

She opened to a clean spread.

On the left, she wrote what any competent doctor might read.

**Patient exhibits artificial overcirculation following stimulant exposure. External support may temporarily mask underlying vitality depletion.**

Ordinary enough.

On the right, beneath it, she wrote:

**3○ — not black market. State scale.**

That line was for Luo.

A stranger would see three circles and assume a dosage category.

Luo would remember burned garlic, ruined fish and the first private code they built together.

He would know it meant the visible explanation was false.

Yun continued.

**2 paired circles / 10 marks.**

To anyone else: circulation notes.

To Luo: ten ranked martial seats operating inside one command structure.

**Seat 2 = capture specialist. Higher line above.**

Shunto.

Tsubasa.

Then the troop estimate.

She disguised it as a body-weight calculation.

**Mass >200,000 units. Minimum estimate. Distributed system; do not assume single chamber.**

Luo would understand “chamber” as formation or deployment environment, not anatomy.

She smiled despite herself.

He would complain that she was abusing notation.

Then he would decode every line.

Yun turned the page.

The next section held Isgard.

She used the old anatomical nickname they once gave the northern circulation branch in a cadaver study.

**Cold limb.**

Ridiculous.

Private.

Memorable.

**Cold limb repeatedly referenced in transport + trauma preparation. External objective probable.**

She paused.

Probable.

Not confirmed yet.

That distinction mattered.

She added:

**Need clock. Need assembled scale. Then leave.**

The last two words pressed harder into the paper.

Then leave.

Yun finished the troop-rotation sequence and noticed the empty bottom margin.

There was room for one more line.

Not enough for anything useful.

Which was why she used it.

She wrote three apparently unrelated medical marks beneath the last supply notation:

**Low heat. Empty stomach. Repeated diagnostic fixation.**

A Shinsei clerk would read it as a symptom cluster.

A physician might assume it described one of the Axtaya cases.

Luo would know better.

It was an old joke from the second winter they had worked together. He would skip meals while diagnosing difficult patients, then become irritated when his hands shook and insist the problem was inadequate tea.

Yun had once written the same three marks across his breakfast bowl.

Translated properly, they meant only:

**You forgot to eat again.**

Yun looked at the line.

Then added one final notation beside it.

**Treat before recurrence.**

Eat something, idiot.

Her mouth moved very slightly.

Then she went back to the war.

Yun closed the notebook and stared at the cover.

It looked ordinary.

Cheap leather.

Water-stained corner.

One repaired thread along the spine.

If Shunto seized it, he might understand it mattered.

He might even decode parts.

He was too intelligent to assume unreadable meant meaningless.

But decoding the whole structure would require history he did not possess.

He had never watched Luo burn dinner.

He had never heard Yun complain that pulse notation looked like drunk insects.

He did not know which abbreviations came from medicine and which came from years of teasing someone she loved.

That was the strength of the notebook.

Not encryption.

Relationship.

Yun disliked that thought enough to stand up.

Sentiment was becoming expensive.

She packed the notebook into the false bottom of a diagnostic case and left the room.

A coded message was only useful if it moved.

Keeping everything beside her until the perfect escape meant one capture could erase three months.

She needed redundancy.

The first attempt was a merchant courier.

Yun watched him for two days.

He was clean.

Too clean.

His route passed one Shinsei inspection station where letters from foreign travelers were opened under customs authority.

No.

The second was a temple network.

Better.

Pilgrims crossed provincial lines without unusual scrutiny.

But the temple’s northern branch sent all winter traffic through a central registry.

Shunto’s people could query it.

No.

The third was a medical transport service carrying case notes toward coastal teaching hospitals.

Yun followed one wagon across three districts.

The records were sealed, not because Shinsei respected privacy unconditionally, but because medical bureaucracy had become powerful enough that opening every file required authorization and created paperwork.

Paperwork again.

Her favorite enemy.

Her occasional ally.

Yun could not send the whole notebook.

Too valuable.

Too obvious if missing.

So she copied only a narrow section onto clinical sheets.

Enough for Luo to understand one thing if the packet escaped:

Axtaya was state-scale.

Shinsei had ten Paragon seats.

Isgard was being prepared as an external objective.

She did not include the physical sample.

That stayed on her body.

She did not include everything she knew.

That stayed in her head.

Three layers.

Again.

Yun looked once more at the three marks she had copied into the bottom margin.

They were not necessary to the invasion warning.

She had copied them anyway.

If the packet reached Wuyue, Luo would read the troop estimate first.

The Ten Seats second.

Isgard third.

Then, somewhere beneath all the numbers, he would find her telling him to eat.

Yun considered removing it.

Did not.

The packet needed to survive her.

That did not mean every surviving line had to belong to the war.

Yun sealed the pages beneath legitimate dermatology reports purchased from a retired physician.

Then she wrote a destination clinic in Wuyue using a forwarding code Lu Weiran could identify if the package reached alliance trade channels.

Not Luo’s name.

Never Luo’s name.

The packet entered the medical wagon at noon.

Yun watched from a noodle stall across the street.

The driver complained about wheel grease.

A clinic apprentice loaded two crates.

A guard inspected the transport seal.

The wagon moved.

Yun did not follow.

Following would turn a good route into her route.

She finished the noodles instead.

They were terrible.

Luo would have said they needed more vinegar.

Yun added vinegar.

Still terrible.

She ate them anyway.

Three streets later, she noticed the first tail.

Not Shinsei military.

A boy carrying folded newspapers.

He appeared twice within six minutes.

Could be coincidence.

Then a woman with red cuffs crossed the same intersection after Yun changed direction.

Could still be coincidence.

Then a municipal cart stopped beside a side lane Yun had used before.

Not coincidence.

Shunto was pressing routes again.

Yun entered a pharmacy.

Bought fever bark.

Exited through the front.

No panic.

No sudden turn.

The newspaper boy remained outside.

Good.

He expected her to detect him eventually.

That meant he was not the real tail.

Yun kept walking.

At a polished window she saw the reflection of a man two shopfronts behind her.

Plain coat.

No package.

Hands covered.

He had been at the noodle stall.

There.

She smiled faintly.

Shunto had learned.

The visible tail was bait.

The actual surveillance stood farther back and avoided contact.

Yun crossed into a crowded spice market.

The man did not follow directly.

Another person took the angle.

Route officers separated from capture officers.

Exactly as the directive had said.

He was building a net without touching it.

Yun’s smile disappeared.

The packet was already moving north-west.

She had done what she could.

Now the priority was not to lead Shunto toward it.

She turned east.

Away from the medical road.

Away from home.

Every instinct objected.

Good.

That meant the choice was probably correct.

She walked deeper into Shinrin while the first piece of Luo’s private language travelled in the opposite direction.

## Chapter 336 — What Paper Cannot Carry

Yun burned her own notes for two hours.

Not the notebook.

Everything around it.

Draft calculations.

Copied supply numbers.

Partial names.

Route sketches.

Clinic schedules.

A badly drawn harbor map she had spent an entire night reconstructing.

The brazier ate three months one page at a time.

The ash made her angry.

That was useful too.

Anger kept memory sharp.

She sat cross-legged before the brazier and rebuilt the investigation aloud.

“Shinsei Guild controls civil government, military regulation, judiciary and strategic infrastructure.”

Burn.

“Axtaya originated from legitimate medical stimulant research with safeguards later stripped or overridden in military application.”

Burn.

“Three-times Redline output exists inside the wider program.”

Burn.

“Cost includes future vitality. Physical damage and spent lifespan are not the same injury.”

Burn.

“Ten ranked Paragon seats.”

Burn.

“Shunto Takamori. No.2. High Paragon. Capture specialist. Adaptive procedure.”

Burn.

“Tsubasa Kurokawa. No.1. Stronger than Shunto. Exact martial mechanics unknown.”

Burn.

“Wider trained apparatus exceeds two hundred thousand by conservative lower-bound reconstruction.”

Burn.

“Peak-Marquis-class density abnormally high within trained elite cohorts.”

Burn.

“External preparation points north.”

The harbor sketch curled.

“Isgard.”

Flame reached the ink.

Yun closed her eyes.

Then repeated everything without looking.

Again.

Again.

Again.

By the sixth repetition, she reversed the order.

By the eighth, she started from the troop estimate and worked backward to Axtaya.

By the tenth, she removed proper nouns and rebuilt them from relationships.

If she woke concussed, could she still remember?

If Shunto took the notebook, could she still speak?

If she lost the sample, could she still explain how to identify it?

Paper was evidence.

Memory was continuity.

Neither replaced the other.

Yun picked up the small sealed capillary hidden in the wrist brace.

Physical proof.

She tapped the diagnostic case.

Coded notebook.

Then touched two fingers to her temple.

Memory.

Three layers.

She had become irritatingly methodical.

Luo would be proud.

Then furious.

Probably both.

A knock came from below.

Not the landlord.

Different weight.

Different pause.

Yun went still.

A man’s voice drifted up the stairs.

“Municipal fire inspection.”

Of course.

She looked at the brazier.

For one second the universe became too stupid to respect.

Yun covered the ash.

The knock came again.

“Inspection.”

The landlord answered below.

“You came last month.”

“Updated district requirement.”

Shunto.

Not personally.

His pressure.

Yun moved to the rear window.

Two floors down, the alley appeared empty.

That meant nothing now.

She checked the roofline.

No visible figures.

Also nothing.

The landlord argued.

Yun had perhaps thirty seconds.

She took the notebook.

Took the brace.

Left the clothes.

Left the coins.

Left the false marriage.

Poor imaginary husband.

They had not even reconciled.

She opened the window and climbed onto the rain gutter.

A patrolman stepped into the far end of the alley.

Not looking up.

Blocking one direction.

Yun looked the other way.

A vegetable cart stopped across the exit.

Too perfectly.

Shunto had learned not to put soldiers where a fugitive expected soldiers.

Fine.

She climbed.

The roof tiles were wet.

Her wrist complained immediately.

Yun ignored it.

Behind her, boots entered the room.

No shouting.

No one cried “fugitive.”

That told her everything.

This was not a broad search.

It was a confirmation sweep.

They already suspected the room.

Yun crossed two roofs, dropped through a textile loft and walked out carrying a bolt of cheap blue cloth like an employee.

A route officer saw her.

His eyes paused.

Not long enough.

Yun kept walking.

He did not pursue.

Smart.

He touched two fingers to his belt instead.

Signal.

Yun turned at the next lane.

A bell rang somewhere behind her.

Not city alarm.

One note.

Then another farther ahead.

The net was passing information without rushing.

Yun accelerated.

Two more corners.

A clinic yard.

Laundry line.

Drainage stair.

She dropped under the street and entered the old maintenance channel beneath a public fountain.

Nobody followed.

That was worse.

She waited in darkness.

Water ran past her boots.

Ten minutes.

Twenty.

Thirty.

Nothing.

Shunto was not trying to catch her there.

He was watching where she came out.

Yun leaned against the stone.

The investigation had reached the point where every escape taught him another piece of her map.

Fine.

Then she would change maps.

She remained underground until night.

When she finally emerged, she did so three districts away through a maintenance hatch inside a closed brewery.

The route cost her the school accountant’s room permanently.

Another safehouse gone.

Another piece of paper burned.

But the three things that mattered remained.

Proof.

Notebook.

Memory.

Yun walked into the dark reciting names, numbers and relationships beneath her breath.

What paper could not carry, she would.

## Chapter 337 — No Needless Contact

Shunto found her in a cemetery.

Yun considered that rude.

She had chosen the cemetery because nobody with normal priorities visited after rain.

The graves sat on a hillside outside the western district, old stone markers leaning beneath cedar trees.

No markets.

No customs posts.

No narrow houses with neighbors who remembered faces.

For once, the dead offered excellent operational security.

Yun had come to meet a former military orderly who sold obsolete transport seals.

The man did not arrive.

Shunto did.

He stepped between two cedar trunks thirty paces downhill.

Alone.

At least visibly.

His hooked blades remained sheathed.

His hands were gloved.

His collar was high.

His circulation was quiet enough that Yun could not read the old dominant reroute she had poisoned before.

He had changed again.

Yun looked around.

No movement among the graves.

That meant there was definitely movement among the graves.

“You are late,” she said.

Shunto’s mouth twitched.

“For your meeting?”

“So you intercepted him.”

“Yes.”

“Alive?”

“Yes.”

Yun believed him.

Kindness had nothing to do with it; killing a transport-seal broker would waste information.

“You disappoint me,” she said.

“I have had three months to become comfortable with that.”

Rain slipped from cedar needles.

Neither moved closer.

That was the first thing Yun noticed.

Before, Shunto always reduced distance.

His style wanted structure through contact.

A wrist.

Elbow.

Shoulder.

Hip.

One controlled point becoming two.

Two becoming a lock.

Now he stayed thirty paces away.

No needless contact.

Yun reached slowly into her sleeve.

Shunto did not flinch.

“Needle?” he asked.

“Handkerchief.”

“Liar.”

“Reasonable guess.”

She withdrew an empty hand.

Shunto said, “You have been looking for northern transport seals.”

Yun said nothing.

“You stopped using coastal routes.”

Still nothing.

“You abandoned two eastbound channels after we changed health screening.”

Yun’s expression stayed flat.

Shunto watched her carefully.

Not her face.

Her breathing.

Her weight distribution.

Her hands.

He had stopped trying to intimidate her into mistakes.

He was collecting them.

“You want out,” he said.

“Everyone eventually does.”

“Three months ago you wanted information.”

“I still do.”

“Now you want both.”

Yun smiled faintly.

“Brilliant.”

Shunto ignored it.

“I can end this without injury.”

“There it is.”

“You surrender the material you took, submit to questioning and stop resisting.”

“Then what?”

“That depends on what you know.”

“No.”

Shunto’s eyes hardened.

“You did not even consider it.”

“I considered it months ago.”

“Before you knew what we were.”

“Especially before.”

A gust moved through the cedars.

Yun felt the net then.

Not killing intent.

Placement.

Four faint qi signatures high on the slope.

Three below.

Perhaps more suppressed.

None close.

Route control.

Shunto had placed people where her escapes would become choices rather than reactions.

He had adapted to poison by refusing to become the center of the encounter.

Yun respected that.

She also hated him for it.

“You learned,” she said.

“So did you.”

“Mine was more fun.”

“You disabled my arm.”

“Yes.”

“For eleven minutes.”

Yun’s gaze sharpened.

He knew the duration.

Of course he did.

Shunto continued.

“I no longer need to touch you to make you choose a route.”

There.

The real threat.

Yun looked uphill.

A narrow shrine path.

Bad.

Downhill, a stone stair toward the western road.

Worse.

East, cedar terraces.

Likely watched.

West, old burial wall and drainage beyond.

Too obvious.

Which meant perhaps intentionally less guarded.

Or intentionally more.

Three months had turned every escape route into an argument with herself.

Yun exhaled.

Then threw a poison needle directly at Shunto’s face.

He neither caught it nor wasted motion on a dramatic dodge. He stepped half a pace aside.

The needle passed.

Exactly.

No contact.

Yun moved at the same instant.

Not west.

Not uphill.

She ran straight at him.

For the first time, Shunto’s expression changed.

Good.

His surrounding officers expected flight away from the strongest point.

Yun chose the strongest point because Shunto’s new doctrine required him not to give her contact cheaply.

He shifted back.

One step.

That was all she needed.

Yun slammed her palm into the ground.

The powder under her sleeve was not a toxin.

It was lime, grave dust and crushed cedar resin.

A filthy opaque cloud exploded between them.

Shunto did not enter it.

Smart.

Yun did not remain inside it.

Smarter.

She crossed his previous position, used a headstone as a step and cleared the low burial wall behind him.

Two route officers appeared immediately.

Not capture specialists.

Covered skin.

Short weapons.

No exposed grips.

They did not engage close.

They drove her left.

Yun let them.

Then regretted it.

A third team waited below the wall.

Shunto had built layers.

No unnecessary contact.

No single close fighter she could contaminate and use as a hole.

Pressure from angles.

Movement denial.

Yun slid beneath one sweeping staff, kicked loose stone into another officer’s knee and jumped into the drainage channel.

The channel forked.

Left went toward a village.

Right toward an old cremation road.

She chose right.

A bell sounded behind her.

One note.

The teams did not follow immediately.

Yun ran harder.

She emerged an hour later beyond the western farms.

No pursuit.

For the first time that day, she stopped.

Then she saw the road marker.

Her stomach sank.

The cremation road joined the same northern medical corridor she had been saving as one of her last two viable exits.

Shunto had not caught her.

He had done something worse.

He had watched which way she chose under pressure.

Yun turned slowly.

Far behind, beyond rain and trees, no figure was visible.

He did not need to be.

The northern medical corridor was compromised now.

Not necessarily blocked.

Compromised.

She could no longer trust that he did not know she valued it.

Yun closed her eyes.

Two exits had become one and a half.

Maybe one.

She laughed once without humor.

“Congratulations.”

The empty road did not answer.

Shunto had returned without touching her once.

And still taken another piece of the map away.
