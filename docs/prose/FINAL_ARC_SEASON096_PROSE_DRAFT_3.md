## Chapter 318 — Aya’s Old Safeguards

The first time Yun saw Aya Katsuragi’s name, it was attached to a warning nobody had obeyed.

She found it in a district medical archive that smelled of dust, boiled cloth and the particular boredom of records nobody expected a foreign merchant to care about.

The clerk had sent her there because one of Axtaya’s precursor ingredients appeared in an old emergency-tonic registry.

“Third cabinet,” he said without looking up. “Circulatory supports. Pre-unification formats are red-tagged.”

Yun paused.

[[speaker:yun]]“Pre-unification?”

“Old standards.”

He dipped his brush again.

“Half of them are useless. Don’t copy dosages without checking current tables.”

That was excellent advice.

It was also exactly what Yun planned to ignore.

The third cabinet contained forty years of medical thinking in increasingly standardized handwriting.

Early entries were inconsistent.

Clan notation.

Local herb names.

Dosages written as “one spoon” or “until the pulse steadies,” which made Yun want to resurrect the physicians responsible and poison them personally.

Then the records changed.

Pulse thresholds became numerical.

Contraindications appeared.

Body-mass ranges.

Meridian-damage exclusions.

Mandatory observation periods.

A conservative emergency stimulant protocol was marked with a seal she recognized from newer documents.

**KATSURAGI, AYA — CLINICAL REVISION.**

Yun read the page twice.

The compound was not Axtaya.

Not yet.

It was a controlled emergency circulatory stimulant designed for trauma, shock and situations where a wounded cultivator needed enough temporary output to survive evacuation or surgery.

The expected increase was modest.

Twenty percent.

Thirty at the extreme.

And the safeguards were everywhere.

Do not administer to patients with unstable meridian tears.

Do not repeat within the same day without direct physician review.

Stop immediately if pulse irregularity exceeds threshold.

No martial-performance use.

No administration for training or rank advancement.

The final line had been underlined twice.

Yun almost laughed.

Nothing about it was funny; she had seen what the descendant of this medicine looked like.

A Peak Marquis vomiting blood after forcing three times his ordinary throughput.

She copied the formula structure into her notes.

Not the full recipe.

The logic.

Then she moved forward through the archive.

Five years later, Aya’s name appeared again.

A stronger variant.

Still medical.

Still screened.

A note referenced experimental use in disaster extraction teams.

The safeguards remained.

Another set of records followed.

Military review.

Operational resilience testing.

The language changed.

Patient became subject.

Emergency evacuation became short-duration performance preservation.

Risk became acceptable attrition.

Yun’s eyes narrowed.

Aya’s signature disappeared from several later documents.

Not all.

Some carried her technical comments in the margin.

**Insufficient recovery interval.**

**Do not generalize healthy-volunteer tolerance to wounded troops.**

**Reject repeat dosing without physician presence.**

Then newer forms cited the same data while omitting the objections.

Yun sat very still.

This mattered.

It would have been easier if Aya Katsuragi had simply designed Axtaya as a weapon and smiled while signing the order.

Easier to explain.

Easier to hate.

The documents suggested something worse in a different way.

A medical system had learned how to help a body borrow a little strength safely enough to survive catastrophe.

Then an institution had kept asking how much more it could borrow.

Yun turned another page.

A later table listed performance brackets.

One-point-five.

Two.

Three.

The last row carried a black border.

No complete formula.

No explicit lifespan estimate in the public archive.

But the structure was there.

Yun recognized the progression immediately.

She copied only the headings.

The clerk passed behind her.

“Find what you need?”

Yun covered the page with one hand and looked up.

[[speaker:yun]]“Mostly old mistakes.”

He smiled without interest.

“Those are popular.”

[[speaker:yun]]“Does Katsuragi still practice?”

That got more attention.

The clerk looked at the name.

“Aya Katsuragi?”

[[speaker:yun]]“Yes.”

He stared at Yun as though she had asked whether rain still fell.

“She’s one of the Ten.”

Yun kept her face neutral.

[[speaker:yun]]“The Ten?”

The clerk’s expression became mildly suspicious.

“You really are foreign.”

[[speaker:yun]]“So I’m told.”

He shook his head.

“Shinsei Ten. Ranked officers.”

[[speaker:yun]]“Physician and officer?”

“Katsuragi is medical command.”

[[speaker:yun]]“What rank?”

“Seven.”

Yun’s pulse did not change.

Her thoughts did.

One of Shinsei’s ten highest officers was a physician whose old work sat at the root of Axtaya.

The clerk walked away.

Yun closed the archive ledger.

She had come to Shinrin expecting to trace a pharmaceutical chain.

Now the chain was climbing directly into the state.

And the woman whose name stood at its medical beginning was not hidden in a basement.

She was No.7.

Yun wrote one sentence in Luo’s private notation.

**Origin appears medical. Militarization later. Do not assume creator equals final doctrine.**

Then, beneath it:

**Aya Katsuragi — Shinsei Ten, No.7.**

The shape of the problem finally rose above ordinary crime.

## Chapter 319 — Do Not Let Him Touch You

Yun first saw Shunto Takamori smiling at a fruit seller.

That was unfortunate.

Villains were easier when they entered through smoke.

He stood beneath a striped awning in a river-market district, one hand resting loosely at his side while the seller argued that the peaches were worth twice the posted price because they had survived an unseasonable storm.

Shunto listened seriously.

Then bought six.

Yun watched from across the street through the reflection in a lacquered signboard.

She did not know his name yet.

She knew three other things.

He was strong.

He was looking for somebody.

And the two men behind him were pretending not to be soldiers badly enough that she wanted to grade them.

Yun turned away before the reflection could become mutual.

Her merchant identity had survived more than three weeks.

Longer than planned.

That alone was beginning to worry her.

She had not stayed from indecision. Every new layer of the investigation had made immediate departure less useful than one more day of evidence.

First Axtaya’s institutional connection.

Then Shinsei’s true governmental role.

Then the medical records.

Now somebody had started asking questions about a foreign trader visiting archives she did not technically need.

Yun entered a fabric shop.

Bought nothing.

Exited through the rear lane.

The two plain-clothed soldiers appeared at the far end three minutes later.

Better than she had hoped.

Worse than she wanted.

She cut left.

The lane narrowed between dye houses. Steam rolled from an open vat. Indigo water ran along a gutter.

Yun dipped two fingers into the runoff as she passed.

Not enough to poison anyone.

Enough to change what would happen if certain compounds met wet skin later.

At the next corner, she brushed a wall.

Another trace.

Then a door latch.

Then nothing for thirty paces.

Make patterns only if you want people to learn them.

She heard one pursuer enter the lane.

Then stop.

Good.

The second kept moving.

Less good.

Yun climbed a low storage wall, crossed a roof and dropped into a courtyard where three women were sorting bean pods.

They stared at her.

Yun held up a coin.

[[speaker:yun]]“Front door?”

One woman pointed.

Yun handed her the coin and left.

The street beyond was crowded.

Perfect.

She moved with a group of porters.

Then heard a man behind her say, conversationally:

“Foreign physicians walk very quickly here.”

Yun did not turn.

[[speaker:yun]]“Foreign physicians are charged by the hour.”

The voice came closer.

“Merchant, then?”

[[speaker:yun]]“Whichever permits are cheaper.”

A hand touched her elbow.

Lightly.

That was the mistake.

Not his.

Hers.

Yun’s arm went numb from wrist to shoulder before she could complete the turn.

No strike.

No crushing grip.

Two fingers had found a circulation junction with terrifying precision.

She drove her heel backward.

The man was no longer there.

Her kick passed through empty space.

Another touch landed near her shoulder blade.

Her right leg weakened.

Yun’s mind became perfectly clear.

Do not let him touch you.

She dropped instead of trying to stand.

The sudden collapse changed the angle of his next contact.

Her left hand struck the street.

Powder burst from inside her sleeve.

Not poison.

Pepper ash and powdered chalk.

The crowd shouted and scattered.

The man withdrew one step rather than breathe blindly.

Yun rolled under a handcart.

Kicked the axle pin loose.

The wheel collapsed sideways.

A basket of river eels hit the street.

Chaos became available.

She used it.

By the time the numbness in her arm began to fade, Yun was three alleys away and wearing somebody else’s brown overshawl.

She did not stop until the district bell rang noon.

Then she entered a public bathhouse, paid for a private wash stall and sat fully clothed on the wooden bench.

Her right hand trembled once.

Yun flexed it.

The touch had not merely numbed tissue.

It had interrupted local qi response with almost surgical control.

A capture art.

Not assassination.

That distinction mattered.

Whoever he was, he had wanted her functional.

Yun reached inside her sleeve.

The powder packet was gone.

Expected.

Her fingers found the second packet.

Then the third.

Still there.

She breathed out.

In the market, the smiling man had bought peaches.

In the alley, he had almost disabled half her body with three gentle contacts.

Yun opened her notebook.

She did not write his face.

Faces could be lost.

She wrote the method.

**Capture specialist. Contact-based disabling. Extremely precise. Superior tier likely Paragon.**

Then underlined:

**DO NOT LET HIM TOUCH YOU.**

For a moment she considered sending the warning home immediately.

But she still had no name.

No rank.

No proof that he represented more than one powerful investigator inside a very large state.

Yun closed the book.

The three-week deadline had already passed.

That fact felt heavier than it had yesterday.

## Chapter 320 — The Third Week Ends

Luo Wen counted days badly after twenty-one.

Before that, he counted perfectly.

Day fourteen had been reasonable.

Day seventeen, annoying.

Day nineteen, worth checking the route board twice.

Day twenty-one was the agreed threshold.

On day twenty-two, he began lying to himself.

Ships were late.

Merchant traffic changed.

Yun might have found a useful lead.

The third-week contact window had never been a blood oath.

On day twenty-four, Luo stopped sleeping properly.

On day twenty-six, Sera found him in the laboratory before dawn, sitting beside a lamp that had burned out hours earlier.

He was holding Yun’s second notebook.

The one she had not taken.

Sera leaned against the doorframe.

[[speaker:sera]]“Anything?”

[[speaker:luo]]“No.”

[[speaker:sera]]“You’ve checked all drops?”

[[speaker:luo]]“Lu has.”

[[speaker:sera]]“Ports?”

[[speaker:luo]]“Twice.”

[[speaker:sera]]“Isgard route?”

[[speaker:luo]]“No contact.”

Sera entered and sat opposite him.

The laboratory looked wrong without Yun.

Not emptier.

Less balanced.

Her side of the workbench still held three labelled packets Luo had refused to move.

One label was crooked.

He had been staring at it for days.

Sera followed his gaze.

[[speaker:sera]]“You can straighten it.”

[[speaker:luo]]“No.”

[[speaker:sera]]“She’ll survive you touching paper.”

[[speaker:luo]]“That isn’t the point.”

Sera nodded.

She knew.

[[speaker:sera]]“Say it.”

Luo looked at her.

[[speaker:luo]]“What?”

[[speaker:sera]]“The thing you’ve been refusing to say because saying it feels like making it true.”

His jaw tightened.

For several seconds he said nothing.

Then:

[[speaker:luo]]“She’s overdue.”

Sera waited.

Luo looked down at the notebook.

[[speaker:luo]]“Something is wrong.”

Luo had finally said it aloud.

Not panic.

Not grief.

A diagnosis.

Sera had known Yun was overdue too. Lu had quietly increased observation at every relevant port four days earlier. Quiet Hand cells had begun tracking inbound foreign medical cargo for any sign of an emergency marker.

But Luo was the first person with the right to say it aloud.

“What do you want to do?” Sera asked.

[[speaker:luo]]“Go.”

[[speaker:sera]]“Where?”

[[speaker:luo]]“Shinrin.”

[[speaker:sera]]“Which city?”

[[speaker:luo]]“I don’t know.”

[[speaker:sera]]“Which route?”

[[speaker:luo]]“The one she used.”

[[speaker:sera]]“If it’s compromised?”

[[speaker:luo]]“I’ll change it.”

[[speaker:sera]]“How will you know where?”

Luo looked at her.

Anger flashed there.

Not at Sera exactly.

At the questions for continuing to be sensible.

[[speaker:luo]]“She said three weeks.”

[[speaker:sera]]“I know.”

[[speaker:luo]]“She said if she found nothing, she’d come back.”

[[speaker:sera]]“I know.”

[[speaker:luo]]“She would contact me.”

[[speaker:sera]]“I know.”

His hand closed around the notebook hard enough to whiten his knuckles.

Sera did not tell him to calm down.

She hated when people did that to her.

Instead she said, “Then we act like she missed the window.”

Luo’s eyes sharpened.

[[speaker:luo]]“Meaning?”

[[speaker:sera]]“Lu expands the route search. Xie’s shipping ledger gets cross-checked against every Shinrin medical export we can reach. Isgard gets a quiet request to watch southern arrivals without explaining why. Quaint cells start looking for Yun’s emergency marks.”

[[speaker:luo]]“And me?”

[[speaker:sera]]“You stay where she expected her evidence to be understood.”

His face hardened.

[[speaker:luo]]“Sera.”

[[speaker:sera]]“That was her reason.”

[[speaker:luo]]“I know what she said.”

[[speaker:sera]]“Then don’t make her wrong because you’re scared.”

That hurt him.

Sera saw it.

She did not take it back.

Luo looked away.

For a long moment the only sound was water dripping in the wash basin.

Then the door opened.

Lu entered.

He carried two sheets of paper.

Luo stood so fast the chair scraped.

Lu raised one hand.

[[speaker:luweiran]]“Not contact.”

Luo stopped.

[[speaker:luo]]“What?”

[[speaker:luweiran]]“A route change.”

Lu put the papers down.

[[speaker:luweiran]]“One of the merchant intermediaries on Yun’s original chain stopped using its registered southern warehouse eight days ago. Cargo is still moving under the same invoices, but the physical storage address changed.”

Sera frowned.

[[speaker:sera]]“To where?”

[[speaker:luweiran]]“Interior Shinrin.”

Luo’s breathing slowed.

Relief had nothing to do with it. His mind finally had something to work on.

[[speaker:luo]]“Why?”

[[speaker:luweiran]]“Unknown.”

[[speaker:luo]]“Can we follow it?”

[[speaker:luweiran]]“From here? Slowly.”

“Xie?” Sera asked.

Lu’s mouth flattened.

[[speaker:luweiran]]“He says he is not employed.”

[[speaker:sera]]“That was not my question.”

[[speaker:luweiran]]“He is already checking the port.”

Luo almost laughed.

It came out as air.

Sera stood.

[[speaker:sera]]“Good. We do this properly.”

Luo looked at her.

[[speaker:luo]]“Properly may be too slow.”

[[speaker:sera]]“Yes.”

That answer surprised him.

Sera continued.

[[speaker:sera]]“But blind is slower if it gets more people caught.”

He said nothing.

She touched two fingers to the notebook in his hand.

[[speaker:sera]]“Yun knew what she was doing when she left you here.”

Luo looked down.

[[speaker:luo]]“I know.”

[[speaker:sera]]“Trust that part of her while we find the rest.”

He closed his eyes once.

Then nodded.

Day twenty-six became day twenty-seven.

No message came.

By then, nobody at the Quaint Teahouse called the delay ordinary.
