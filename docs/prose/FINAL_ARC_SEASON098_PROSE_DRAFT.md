## Chapter 331 — Safehouses Become Calendar Marks

Yun stopped counting days by sunrise.

Sunrise was too generous.

It suggested continuity.

Morning, noon, evening. A world in which a person slept in the same room long enough for light to cross the same wall twice.

Yun counted time by safehouses instead.

The room above the fishmonger had lasted nine days.

The dye worker’s back loft lasted six.

The abandoned bathhouse lasted three because one of Shunto’s route officers noticed steam where there should have been none.

The widow near South Lock let Yun sleep behind sacks of winter barley for eleven nights, which became the closest thing she had to luxury.

Then the widow’s nephew received a new municipal inspection assignment.

Yun left before dawn.

The nephew had not betrayed her. He might simply be ordered to inspect his own building and become guilty without ever choosing it.

That was the problem with hiding under a government this complete.

Shinsei did not need everyone to be loyal.

It only needed ordinary systems to keep functioning.

Permits.

Clinics.

Food allotments.

Street repairs.

Harbor checks.

Temple registries.

Work rosters.

Every useful thing was also a way to notice someone who did not belong.

Yun had admired that once.

A part of her still did.

That part was becoming inconvenient.

She crossed a morning market under a rain cloak that had not existed when she entered Shinrin.

The weather had changed.

That bothered her more than it should have.

When she first arrived, the heat had been heavy enough that vendors covered fruit with wet cloth.

Now the mornings were cold.

Chestnut smoke replaced melon peel in the gutters.

Children wore padded jackets to school.

A tea seller she had passed every third day had changed his menu board from iced plum tea to ginger broth.

The third month announced itself in small humiliations.

Yun bought the ginger broth.

The seller handed it over without looking closely at her.

Good.

She had changed too.

Her hair was shorter because long hair was something another person could grab.

A thin scar crossed the underside of her jaw where Shunto’s sleeve-hook had caught skin two encounters ago.

Her left wrist still ached in rain.

That injury was not from him.

It was from dropping through a tiled roof because the staircase below had become a capture lane.

She had lost four poison kits.

Two were abandoned deliberately.

One had been seized.

One was now buried beneath a shrine courtyard with enough false contamination around it that even Shunto’s revised procedure should take time to clear.

Should.

Yun had stopped trusting that word.

She drank the broth while walking.

No stopping at corners.

No repeating the same lane twice in one week.

No entering any building whose rear exit she had not already seen.

Shunto had changed her habits.

She had changed his.

Neither of them could afford to admit how much.

At a cloth stall ahead, two municipal officers checked merchant seals.

Not Shinsei military.

Not search teams.

Ordinary bureaucracy.

Yun turned before they looked up.

That was how the last month had gone.

Not dramatic pursuit every day.

Pressure.

Shunto did not need to chase her through rooftops if he could make rooftops the only place she still felt safe.

He had stopped raiding whole districts.

Instead, food clerks began asking better questions.

Clinic records were reviewed faster.

Foreign travel permissions received quiet second checks.

Rooms rented in cash drew attention after three nights instead of ten.

Courier houses were not closed.

They were watched.

He was not hunting where she was.

He was shrinking where she could be.

Yun hated the elegance of it.

She entered a public bathhouse through the women’s side, changed cloaks in a laundry room, exited through a service gate and crossed two canal bridges before heading toward her current room.

Current was optimistic.

The room had belonged to a dismissed school accountant who left the city after a tax dispute.

The landlord believed Yun was his cousin.

The landlord also believed the cousin was married.

Yun had therefore invented a husband who worked on river barges and was rarely home.

The imaginary man had acquired a name, three bad habits and an old knee injury because the landlord liked conversation.

Luo would have found that funny.

The thought landed without warning.

Yun stopped on the stair.

Danger had nothing to do with the pause. For one second she could see Luo leaning against their clinic table while she explained that her fictional husband snored and lost money at dice.

He would have asked whether the fake husband at least washed his cups.

She would have said no.

Luo would have called the man unsalvageable.

Yun closed her eyes.

The third month.

She had promised three weeks.

Luo would no longer be merely worried.

He would be furious.

Good.

Fury meant he had not decided she was dead.

Yun entered the room and locked the door.

Her evidence occupied almost nothing.

That was deliberate.

Two folded sheets inside the lining of a medical wrapping case.

A list of numeric references disguised as dosage calculations.

Three merchant seals.

One shipping code.

And Luo’s notebook.

The notebook had become the most dangerous object in Shinrin.

Its danger did not come from anyone else reading it completely. Shunto knew she was reading.

Eventually he would understand she was writing too.

Yun set the notebook on the floor.

She drew a map beside it from memory.

Then crossed out another route.

The southern freight gate was gone.

Not closed.

Worse.

Routine security rotation had added a secondary health station there.

Two physicians, four clerks, eight guards and mandatory pulse screening for long-distance travelers.

A system designed to prevent epidemic spread.

A good system.

A system she could no longer safely pass.

Yun stared at the map.

When she entered Shinrin, she had six reasonable ways home.

Then four.

Then three.

Now there were two.

One required forged coastal cargo papers she did not yet have.

The other went north through old medical transport roads toward Isgard and had not been used officially in years.

Both were bad.

Which meant Shunto was winning.

Not the chase.

Space.

She opened the notebook.

At the top of a new page she wrote one line.

**Month three. Exit priority now equal to evidence priority.**

Then she stared at it.

The third month under one government.

The third month of finding things that worked well enough to make the things beneath them harder to hate cleanly.

The third month of watching a state capable of feeding villages also prepare medicine for an invasion.

The third month of being hunted by a man whose competence had become part of the architecture around her.

Yun added a second line.

**Do not stay for perfection. Leave with enough.**

Outside, the landlord knocked.

Yun’s hand went to a hidden needle.

“Cousin?” the woman called. “Your husband sent nothing again.”

Yun looked at the ceiling.

Of course he had not.

[[speaker:yun]]“He is unreliable,” she said.

“I told you river men were useless.”

[[speaker:yun]]“You were right.”

The landlord left satisfied.

Yun almost laughed.

Then a bell rang three streets away.

Not alarm.

Shift change.

She returned to the map.

Safehouses had become calendar marks.

Routes had become a countdown.

Yun could feel the investigation narrowing toward an end.

She had not solved everything. Soon there would simply be nowhere left to stand while solving it.

## Chapter 332 — Ten Seats

The number appeared first on a laundry invoice.

Yun nearly ignored it.

That would have been embarrassing.

The invoice came from a military compound outside the eastern administrative quarter and listed ceremonial uniform cleaning before a state commemoration.

Nothing unusual.

Officers liked clean clothes while ordering other people into dirt.

Yun read the quantities anyway.

Ten formal seat-robes.

Ten paired shoulder insignia.

Ten black-circle clasps.

She frowned.

The same supplier had records from the previous year.

Ten.

The year before that.

Ten.

Not generals.

Seats.

Yun copied the supplier code and left.

The next clue came from a clinic.

A senior military physician complained loudly about having to keep ten separate emergency treatment authorizations on file because “the Seats refuse to standardize their circulation profiles.”

Yun was standing two shelves away pretending to compare wound glue.

Ten again.

She bought the glue.

It was excellent.

That was also annoying.

Over the next five days, ten kept appearing.

Budget compartments.

Restricted residence allotments.

Escort protocols.

A public memorial plaque naming “the Ten Seats of Shinsei” without giving operational details.

A children’s history primer that described them as the ten martial pillars who ended the old clan wars.

That one mattered.

Yun read the page three times.

The language was patriotic, but not absurd.

The old clans had been real.

So had the bloodshed.

The book credited the Ten Seats with imposing common law, breaking private armies and establishing roads between territories that had once taxed each other at spearpoint.

The story explained why civilians respected them.

It did not explain their cultivation.

For that, Yun needed military medicine.

She found it in a trauma triage schedule.

Not names.

Categories.

**Seat-level intervention: Paragon circulation protocol.**

Yun stopped breathing for half a second.

Then kept reading.

One line could be wrong.

Propaganda could inflate titles.

Medical protocols could use prestige language for priority patients.

So she checked another facility.

Then another.

Different districts.

Different forms.

Same classification.

Paragon.

All ten.

Yun sat alone that night in the school accountant’s old room and placed ten small stones in a row.

The first five were river pebbles.

The next four were roof gravel.

The tenth was a dried bean because she ran out of stone.

It looked ridiculous.

That helped.

Ten ranked officers.

Ten Paragons.

She knew what one Paragon meant.

Wuyue had built generations of political logic around the rarity of power at that level.

Isgard had nearly broken itself against fewer than this in the last war.

Shinsei had institutionalized ten of them into one chain of command.

Yun touched the second stone.

Shunto Takamori.

No.2.

High Paragon.

The man who had spent months trying to capture her while adjusting municipal procedure around her poison habits.

She had known he was No.2.

She had not understood what the number sat inside.

Nine other Paragon seats changed the meaning.

Yun moved her finger to the first stone.

No.1.

Tsubasa Kurokawa.

That name took longer.

Shinrin did not hide him.

It did the opposite.

Bridges bore dates from his reconstruction decrees.

Military academies quoted speeches attributed to him.

Veterans spoke of “the First Seat” the way Wuyue soldiers spoke of weather before a campaign: not worship exactly, but as something assumed to exist beyond argument.

The useful information came from people who were not trying to impress anyone.

A retired instructor complaining that modern officers leaned too much on numbers.

A clinic quartermaster explaining why No.2’s emergency reserve could never be stored in the same wing as No.1’s.

A former clan fighter drinking rice liquor behind a stable.

“Takamori catches people,” the old man said.

Yun sat beside him because he had mistaken her for a travelling apothecary.

[[speaker:yun]]“He sounds unpleasant.”

“He is efficient.”

[[speaker:yun]]“Worse.”

The old man laughed.

Yun poured another cup.

[[speaker:yun]]“And First Seat?”

The man’s expression changed.

Not fear.

Memory.

“Different.”

[[speaker:yun]]“How?”

The old man considered.

“You know when someone strong enters a room and everyone notices?”

[[speaker:yun]]“Yes.”

“With Kurokawa, the room notices before you do.”

Not useful mechanically.

Useful socially.

[[speaker:yun]]Yun asked, “Stronger than Takamori?”

The old man looked at her as though she had asked whether winter was colder than rain.

“Of course.”

She had what she could defend and did not ask for secret techniques.

People who possessed those details did not tell them to travelling apothecaries over cheap liquor.

Back in her room, Yun opened Luo’s notebook.

She refused to write ten names merely to make the list look complete. Instead she wrote only what she could defend.

**Shinsei maintains ten ranked Seat officers. Multiple independent medical and administrative records classify Seat-level treatment as Paragon protocol. Treat all ten as Paragon unless disproven.**

Below it:

**Shunto Takamori = Seat 2 / High Paragon.**

And below that:

**Tsubasa Kurokawa = Seat 1. Multiple sources treat strength gap above Takamori as obvious. Exact arts unknown.**

Yun underlined the last sentence.

Exact arts unknown. The uncertainty belonged in the report as firmly as the confirmed ranks.

Their Domains remained unknown, as did how the ten fought together, which of them were stationed near the capital, and whether every seat would join an invasion. Yun left those gaps visible instead of filling them with confidence she had not earned.

Fear was not permission to invent intelligence.

Yun leaned back against the wall.

Ten stones sat beside her knee.

Nine proper stones and one bean.

She looked at them for a long time.

Then picked up the bean.

[[speaker:yun]]“Embarrassing,” she told it.

The bean remained professionally silent.

Yun put it back.

A laugh escaped her before she could stop it.

It hurt her ribs.

Good.

Pain meant she was still capable of finding something stupid.

She closed the notebook.

Ten Paragons did not change the mission into defeating Shinsei.

It changed the mission into making sure somebody outside Shinrin understood what defeating Shinsei would actually require.

## Chapter 333 — Two Hundred Thousand Is Not a Rumor

Armies lied beautifully.

Payroll did not.

Payroll could be hidden.

Split.

Reclassified.

Moved through civil ministries.

But somewhere, eventually, someone had to count mouths.

Yun began with rice.

Rice was not dramatic. Soldiers still had to eat it.

A northern grain authority published quarterly storage allocations for disaster planning. The document was public enough that nobody considered it dangerous.

Yun copied three numbers.

Then she found a military transport schedule and copied four more.

Then clinic procurement.

Boot leather.

Training oil.

Cold-weather blankets.

Arrow shafts.

Replacement spear heads.

Laundry soap.

Salt.

The boring body of a state.

One number meant nothing.

Fifty numbers began to argue.

She spent nine days making them argue consistently.

The first estimate came out to one hundred sixty thousand.

Yun stared at it.

Too low.

She did not want a bigger threat; clinic consumption simply implied more wounded trainees than the payroll category could produce.

She checked the classification code.

Reserve units were paid through provincial security budgets.

Yun swore softly.

Three more days.

She added provincial security grain.

Removed civilian garrison staff.

Adjusted for family rations where barracks housed dependents.

Estimated noncombatant logistics crews conservatively.

Removed naval dock laborers.

Added rotating elite training cohorts.

The total passed two hundred thousand.

Yun stopped.

Then did it again.

Different method.

This time she started with boots.

The answer was higher.

She threw that sheet away because boot replacement assumptions were unreliable.

Third method.

Medical throughput.

A soldier did not visit a clinic every month.

But large training systems produced predictable injury patterns.

Shinsei’s records were unusually clean because Aya’s reforms had standardized military medicine years earlier.

That made Yun’s work easier.

Another irony for the collection.

She built ranges instead of pretending precision.

Low estimate.

Medium.

High.

Even the low estimate crossed two hundred thousand trained elites once reserves and regional forces were included.

Yun placed the charcoal down.

The room was silent.

Outside, children argued over a spinning top in the lane.

Two hundred thousand.

More than two hundred thousand people who were not merely conscripts handed a spear at a gate.

Training records showed a floor that would have been considered elite in most regions.

Peak Marquis appeared too often.

Not everywhere.

Not absurdly.

But often enough that the phrase “elite corps” had lost meaning.

Shinsei’s advantage was not only ten Paragons.

It was depth.

A system that could lose strong people and still produce more.

Yun hated systems like that because they were harder to solve with one heroic fight.

She opened Luo’s notebook.

Numbers required discipline.

She wrote:

**Shinsei wider trained apparatus: lower bound >200,000 based on independent grain, provincial security payroll, clinic throughput and equipment rotation. Do not treat as one assembled field army. Includes distributed regional / reserve / support-connected trained elements.**

She underlined **lower bound**.

Then added:

**Peak-Marquis-class density unusually high within trained elite cohorts. Earlier observation confirmed at local level; scale now supported institutionally.**

Yun sat back.

There.

No sensationalism.

No “two hundred thousand monsters.”

No pretending every person in the number could fight Sera.

Just the truth.

The truth was enough.

A soft knock came at the door.

Three taps.

Pause.

One.

The landlord.

Yun closed the notebook.

[[speaker:yun]]“Yes?”

“Your husband has become worse.”

Yun looked toward the ceiling.

[[speaker:yun]]“What did he do now?”

“Nothing. That is the problem. Three months and no letter.”

The fictional marriage was deteriorating faster than Yun’s real one.

[[speaker:yun]]“I will speak to him.”

“When?”

[[speaker:yun]]“When he returns.”

“He is imaginary, isn’t he?”

Yun went still.

Silence.

Then the landlord laughed through the door.

“I’m joking. River men all feel imaginary.”

Footsteps receded.

Yun waited another full minute.

Then exhaled.

Shunto Takamori had nearly caught her four times.

A sixty-year-old landlord had almost killed her with humor.

Unacceptable.

Yun gathered the calculations.

She burned all but one coded page.

The raw numbers were too bulky to carry.

The conclusion had to survive without them.

That meant memorizing the methods too.

Grain.

Payroll.

Clinics.

Equipment.

Different systems converging on the same floor.

She repeated them until she could reconstruct the argument without paper.

Then she returned to the invasion timeline.

Two hundred thousand was not a rumor.

Ten Paragons were not legend.

Axtaya was not black-market cleverness.

And three months ago, Yun had walked into Shinrin expecting to follow a pharmaceutical trail for a few weeks.

Now she was trying to carry the scale of a continent’s war machine out through a door that Shunto was slowly closing.

## Chapter 334 — Proof That Can Survive Her

Yun did not steal a vial.

A vial was too obvious.

Too fragile.

Too easy to confiscate.

Too easy for someone later to claim had been tampered with.

She stole the waste.

The opportunity came from a military clinic that handled failed Axtaya screening batches.

Not Redline combat stock.

Quality control remnants.

Trace solution discarded after potency checks.

The kind of material nobody important wanted because it was too weak to dose a soldier and too contaminated for reuse.

Perfect.

Yun entered disguised as a disposal contractor’s assistant.

The disguise was not elegant.

She carried three buckets and smelled terrible.

Sometimes espionage was mostly accepting indignity before somebody else assigned it.

The disposal room had two guards.

One checked seals.

The other checked faces.

Yun had prepared for seals.

Faces were harder.

She made herself boring.

Eyes down.

Shoulders tired.

Annoyed at the world.

The universal posture of someone underpaid to move medical waste.

The guard barely looked at her twice.

Inside, Yun found what she needed in a ceramic wash tray stained faintly red-brown near the drainage groove.

She did not scrape the whole thing.

That would show.

She used a folded absorbent strip beneath her glove and pressed once along the hidden underside of the tray lip.

Enough residue to test.

Not enough to notice.

Then she washed the bucket badly on purpose.

A worker who cleaned perfectly would be suspicious.

Outside, she walked six blocks before changing direction.

Twelve before discarding the assistant coat.

Twenty before she allowed herself to touch the sample.

The residue alone was not proof.

So Yun prepared it like a physician, not a smuggler.

She split the strip.

One half went into a tiny sealed glass capillary.

The other was dried between two layers of neutral medical gauze with a control section beside it.

She marked both using dosage notation Luo would recognize.

Not “Axtaya.”

Not “Shinrinese military stimulant.”

Just numbers.

If searched, it looked like comparative toxicology samples.

If destroyed, she still had the notebook.

If the notebook was lost, she still had memory.

Three layers.

No single failure could erase everything.

Yun held the capillary up to the light.

There was barely anything inside.

A trace.

Enough for Luo.

Enough for Aya, if the world ever became strange enough for the two of them to compare notes.

Enough to show the substance was real.

Yun wrapped the capillary inside the seam of a physician’s wrist brace.

Then she burned the disposal uniform.

Far to the south, Rhen froze a teapot by touching the table beside it.

The tea itself did not freeze.

Only the pot.

Which somehow made the accident more insulting.

Sera looked at it.

Rhen looked at it.

Neither spoke.

A thin white shell covered the clay from base to lid.

The steam had stopped.

Rhen lifted one hand.

The frost disappeared.

The tea was still hot.
