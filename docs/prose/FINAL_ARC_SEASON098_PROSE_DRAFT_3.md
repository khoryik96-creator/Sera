## Chapter 338 — Four Months

Yun found the clock in six different places.

That was why she trusted it.

The first was a dock-repair calendar.

Three deep-keel transports scheduled for final hull inspection on the same week.

The second was a medical reserve order.

Cold-weather trauma packs moved from production to forward storage twelve days before that inspection window.

The third was a provincial troop rotation.

Two training cohorts were told to complete “external readiness certification” before the same month-end.

The fourth was grain.

Again.

Grain never understood that it was supposed to keep secrets.

A protected northern warehouse shifted from ordinary reserve accounting to accelerated issue schedules on a date that matched the transport calendar.

The fifth was more subtle.

Leave restrictions.

Not cancelled.

Compressed.

Several military districts moved family leave forward by three weeks.

Yun had seen armies do that before.

Let people go home before you stop letting them go home.

The sixth was a clinic staffing memorandum.

**Temporary surge assignment beginning fourth-month readiness window.**

Yun read that line twice.

Then a third time.

Fourth month.

Not “in four months.”

The records were dated from the beginning of the readiness cycle.

She went back through everything.

The earliest Axtaya staging movements she had copied.

The first unusual cold-weather requisitions.

Naval maintenance.

Training rotations.

The cycle had begun shortly after she entered Shinrin.

A four-month readiness program.

And she was now near the end of month three.

Yun sat very still.

The invasion was not some distant contingency.

It was maturing around her.

She drew four lines on the floor with charcoal.

Month one.

Month two.

Month three.

Month four.

The first line held procurement.

The second held training intensification.

The third held forward medical staging, transport consolidation and leave compression.

The fourth held readiness.

She stared at the last line.

If the schedule remained unchanged, Shinsei could begin major deployment within weeks.

Could.

Not would.

She needed to preserve that distinction.

Operational timetables moved.

Weather changed.

Politics intervened.

Commanders delayed.

Commanders accelerated.

But the window existed.

That was enough to make every day she remained inside Shinrin more expensive.

Yun opened Luo’s notebook.

She used pulse-cycle notation.

Four intervals.

The first three marked complete.

The fourth marked with a sharp upward stroke.

Then she wrote:

**Readiness architecture converges on 4-month cycle from initial staging. Currently late interval 3. External action may be weeks, not months. Exact launch order not observed.**

She underlined the last sentence.

Exact launch order not observed.

No master command.

No signed invasion decree.

No convenient parchment saying **ATTACK ISGARD ON THIS DATE**.

Real systems rarely insulted themselves that way.

Yun rebuilt the sequence again.

Axtaya.

Cold-weather medical stock.

Northern transport.

Harbor studies.

Fleet repair.

Elite training density.

Leave compression.

Forward clinic staffing.

Four-month readiness.

She felt the logic settle.

There.

Enough.

For the first time since entering Shinrin, Yun did not need one more layer.

The realization produced no relief.

Only urgency.

She stood.

The room around her belonged to a shuttered instrument maker whose son worked in another province.

Safe for perhaps two more nights.

Maybe less.

Yun packed only what mattered.

Notebook.

Wrist brace sample.

Coins.

Two poison kits.

One ordinary medical kit.

Water.

A change of clothes.

Everything else stayed.

She looked at the map.

The coastal cargo route remained technically possible, but the forged papers she needed had become impossible to obtain without entering two watched districts.

The northern civilian-medical corridor was compromised after the cemetery encounter.

Compromised was not closed.

That distinction had become her entire life.

Yun traced the line north.

Old treatment road.

Disused transfer clinic.

Mountain pass.

Then west toward Isgard-linked territory.

Shunto knew she valued it.

He might not know when she intended to use it.

Unless he did.

Yun rubbed her forehead.

Three months of adaptive pursuit had made certainty impossible.

She needed a route that did not depend on certainty.

She started building one from failure points.

If the clinic was watched, use drainage.

If drainage was sealed, climb the retaining wall.

If the wall was controlled, double back to the old herbal terraces.

If the terraces were watched, abandon the corridor and force west through merchant farms.

Every route had a second route.

Every second route had a cost.

By dawn, Yun had six pages of contingencies.

Then she burned all six.

Memory.

Again.

She slept for ninety minutes.

When she woke, a Shinsei notice had been pasted to the wall across the lane.

Not about her.

Road maintenance.

The northern medical corridor would undergo structural inspection beginning in three days.

Yun stared at the notice.

Three days.

Maybe genuine.

Maybe Shunto.

Maybe both.

It did not matter.

The clock had narrowed again.

Four months for Shinsei.

Three days for Yun.

She folded her cloak.

“Fine.”

The word sounded tired.

She put it on anyway.

## Chapter 339 — No Master File

Yun stopped looking for the order.

That was the final breakthrough.

For weeks, part of her had assumed somewhere inside Shinsei there must be one document that unified everything.

A strategic directive.

A command authorization.

A sealed plan with Tsubasa Kurokawa’s name at the bottom and Isgard written plainly across the top.

There might be.

It no longer mattered.

The invasion existed without it.

Not as a rumor.

As convergence.

Yun sat inside the disused upper floor of a printing shop and rebuilt the case the way she would explain a poison death.

No single symptom proved the cause.

The body did.

Axtaya alone could be medical abuse.

Two hundred thousand trained personnel alone could be national defense.

Ten Paragons alone could be the product of an unusually powerful martial culture.

Isgard maps alone could be contingency planning.

Cold-weather medicine alone could be disaster preparation.

Deep-keel transports alone could be trade.

Leave compression alone could be bureaucracy being annoying.

But all of them together, moving on one clock toward one geography, could not be explained honestly as coincidence.

That was the difference between evidence and a dramatic document.

Evidence survived missing pieces.

Yun wrote nothing.

She no longer needed to.

Instead, she spoke the argument into the empty room.

“State-scale stimulant program.”

Her voice was quiet.

“Military safeguards override original medical limits.”

She touched the wrist brace.

“Physical residue preserved.”

Ten stones would have been useful.

She used printer blocks instead.

Ten small pieces of wood in a row.

“Ten Paragon-ranked Seats.”

Second block.

“Shunto Takamori. No.2. High Paragon. Capture specialist.”

First block.

“Tsubasa Kurokawa. No.1. Stronger. Exact arts unknown.”

She swept the blocks aside.

“Trained apparatus exceeds two hundred thousand by conservative reconstruction.”

She laid down a map from memory.

“External preparation repeatedly aligned with Isgard ports and northern logistics.”

Then four charcoal marks.

“Four-month readiness cycle. Late month three. Launch window may be weeks.”

Yun stopped. She had enough for Sera to act.

Enough for Lu to verify.

Enough for Jin to plan.

Enough for Isgard to understand that northern medical staging was not merely medicine.

Enough for Luo to decode what happened to Axtaya.

Enough.

The word felt unfamiliar.

She had spent three months teaching herself that incomplete intelligence could still be responsible intelligence.

Perfection was not the standard.

Survival with warning was.

Yun packed the printer blocks back into their drawer.

The owner would return someday and wonder why ten letters were out of order.

A small historical inconvenience.

She moved to the window.

Below, Shinsei continued to function.

Street cleaners worked around a broken drain.

A mother argued with a school clerk.

Two soldiers helped an old man lift a handcart over a damaged curb.

A physician crossed the street carrying a red medical case.

This was the country Yun had learned to understand badly enough that she could no longer reduce it to an enemy banner.

That made the invasion worse.

A cruel state was easy to explain.

A competent state choosing coercion while still doing real good was harder.

People would defend it honestly.

Some would oppose it honestly.

Some would never know what had been done in their name.

Yun had seen Aya’s old safeguards.

She had seen clinics that treated civilians without asking whether they loved Shinsei enough.

She had seen children studying roads the Guild really had built.

And beneath all of it, military systems prepared to cross the sea.

Both truths had to survive.

Otherwise the warning would become propaganda instead of intelligence.

Yun turned away from the window.

She checked the route one last time.

The northern medical corridor.

Compromised.

Road inspection in three days.

Possible trap.

Possible genuine maintenance.

No better route remained.

She had one advantage.

Shunto knew she wanted out.

He did not know exactly what she had concluded.

He might assume she still needed more.

Unless he had learned her well enough to know when she would stop.

That possibility sat badly in her stomach.

Yun opened Luo’s notebook to the last coded page.

She added one sentence.

**Enough established. I am leaving now.**

No code.

No joke.

Plain language.

If Luo ever read that line, he deserved one sentence without work.

Yun closed the book.

Outside, a cart passed carrying fresh road barriers north.

She watched it disappear.

Then left the printing shop by the roof.

No master file.

No perfect proof.

No complete understanding.

Only enough truth to change what happened next.

Yun intended to carry it home.

## Chapter 340 — The Open Door

The northern checkpoint had one guard too few.

Yun noticed immediately.

That was problem one.

Problem two was the inspection table.

It stood beneath the awning exactly where regulations required, but the clerk had arranged travel slips in stacks by destination rather than entry order.

Efficient.

Too efficient for a temporary road-maintenance station.

Problem three was the physician.

His sleeves were tied back.

Not unusual.

His gloves were not medical gloves.

They were reinforced at the palms.

Yun walked past without slowing.

The checkpoint did not stop her.

Problem four.

She continued north for half a li.

Nobody followed.

Problem five.

The road narrowed between two retaining walls before opening toward the abandoned transfer clinic.

Yun stopped at the bend.

A normal traveler would have continued.

A hunted person with functioning instincts turned around.

Yun turned around.

The checkpoint behind her had closed.

Not dramatically.

A maintenance cart rolled across half the road.

Two workers began unloading stone.

The clerk remained seated.

The reinforced-glove physician looked at a ledger.

No one stared at Yun.

Shunto.

She felt almost offended by how understated it was.

The open route was the trap.

Good.

She had expected that possibility.

Yun stepped off the road and climbed the eastern retaining slope.

No soldiers appeared.

Problem six.

She reached the herbal terraces.

Empty.

Problem seven.

Too empty.

Three months ago, Shunto would have hidden men in the terrace huts.

Two months ago, he would have controlled the paths.

One month ago, he would have watched the exits and let her move.

Now there was nothing.

Which meant the real capture geometry lay farther out.

Yun looked west.

Merchant farms.

That had been contingency four.

She moved toward them.

A farm bell rang.

Once.

Then twice.

Not alarm.

Ordinary work signal.

Maybe.

Yun changed direction.

South-west toward a drainage cut.

A pair of ox carts entered it from opposite ends at nearly the same time.

Not soldiers.

Not barricades.

Civilian traffic.

Perfectly ordinary.

Perfectly inconvenient.

Yun climbed again.

Her routes were collapsing without anyone visibly attacking her.

That was Shunto’s hand now.

Not force.

Probability.

He had spent three months learning which inconveniences she treated as danger and which she accepted as life.

Yun reached a ridge above the old transfer clinic.

The north road beyond it was open.

Completely.

No carts.

No patrol.

No inspection.

Nothing.

Problem eight.

She crouched behind a stone marker and watched for ten minutes.

A farmer crossed.

Then a woman with a child.

Then two monks.

Normal traffic.

No arrests.

No signals.

Yun waited another ten.

Still nothing.

The route looked safe because it was being allowed to look safe.

She knew that.

The issue was no longer whether it was a trap.

The issue was whether any alternative was less trapped.

Yun looked behind her.

The checkpoint route had closed gently.

The terraces were exposed.

The farms were becoming crowded.

The drainage cut was blocked by ordinary traffic.

Road maintenance would formally close the corridor in three days.

The coastal route required documents she could no longer obtain.

She had enough intelligence.

She had to leave.

Shunto had finally built a problem where recognizing the trap did not solve it.

Yun smiled without humor.

“Very good.”

She took the open road.

Nothing happened for the first li.

Nothing for the second.

At the third, she began to wonder whether the entire purpose was to make her exhaust herself anticipating contact.

At the fourth, she saw the abandoned medical transfer station.

Low tiled roof.

Collapsed eastern wall.

Old ambulance gate hanging crooked.

Beyond it, the road entered foothills.

Once she reached those foothills, terrain multiplied options again.

That was why the station mattered.

Shunto knew it too.

Yun stopped outside.

No qi signatures.

No fresh tracks she could distinguish from travelers.

No disturbed dust along the western wall.

Too clean.

She did not enter.

Instead she circled north around the building.

A wire snapped under her heel.

Not a trap.

A bell.

Tiny.

Almost silent.

Yun moved instantly.

Three figures rose from the grass ahead.

Route officers.

Covered skin.

No close engagement.

She turned west.

Two more appeared on the ridge.

East.

Open.

Too open.

She went east anyway because remaining still was worse.

A powder charge burst against the ground ten paces ahead.

Not poison.

Neutralizing chalk and medicinal absorbent.

Shunto was cleaning the environment before she could weaponize it.

Yun laughed once.

Of course he was.

She threw two needles.

The officers did not catch them.

They yielded space.

No needless contact.

Yun pushed through the gap.

Then Shunto stepped onto the road behind her.

Thirty paces.

Same distance as the cemetery.

Gloves.

High collar.

No exposed skin.

He did not rush.

“Yun Shizhen.”

She kept moving.

“Three months,” he called.

“Your hospitality needs work.”

“You found what you came for.”

That made her stop.

Only for half a heartbeat.

Too much.

Shunto saw it.

Damn.

He did not smile.

That was worse.

“You stopped searching,” he said.

Yun turned to face him.

“How would you know?”

“Your movement changed.”

Of course.

She had stopped circling archives.

Stopped probing supply routes.

Started testing exits.

He had not decoded the intelligence.

He had decoded her.

Shunto said, “Give me the material.”

“No.”

“The notebook.”

Yun’s eyes cooled.

There.

He knew there was a notebook.

Not what it meant.

Enough.

She reached into her sleeve.

Shunto did not move closer.

Yun threw a glass sphere at the ground between them.

Black vapor erupted.

Not lethal.

Dense.

Bitter.

Designed to irritate breathing and hide movement.

Shunto stepped back from the cloud.

Exactly as expected.

Yun ran east.

The officers widened rather than closed.

Exactly as expected.

She crossed the ditch.

Hit the tree line.

Found no one.

For eight seconds, the route was open.

Then her right leg stopped working.

Yun hit the ground hard.

No dart.

No toxin.

No contact.

She looked at her calf.

A thin metal loop had tightened around the boot just above the ankle.

Not enough to restrain her physically.

Enough to carry qi.

The line ran beneath leaves to a buried anchor array.

Shunto had not placed a man in the escape lane.

He had placed a circulation interrupter tuned for the moment she committed weight through it.

Yun cut the loop with a hidden blade.

Her leg returned.

Too late.

The eight-second route had become four.

Route officers moved behind her.

Still no contact.

Yun threw powder into the trees.

Wind carried it away from them.

Prepared airflow.

He had mapped that too.

She changed direction.

A second loop snapped upward.

Yun jumped it.

Third.

She twisted.

Fourth.

Too many.

Not random.

They were positioned along the lines she preferred when pressured.

Three months of observation made physical.

Yun landed on one foot and felt Shunto enter range for the first time.

Not reckless.

Not early.

Now.

After the environment was clean.

After her routes were narrowed.

After her leg had been interrupted once.

After her attention was divided.

His first contact was not her wrist.

She had poisoned that expectation months ago.

He caught the fabric at her upper sleeve with a hooked blade and pulled without touching skin.

Yun cut the sleeve free.

He released instantly.

No contest.

Second contact: the flat of the other hook against her shoulder blade through cloth.

A pulse of qi disrupted one circulation line.

Yun turned and drove a needle toward his throat.

Shunto did not block with his hand.

He turned his blade and let the needle strike metal.

Third contact: boot against the outside of her knee.

Not a kick.

Placement.

Her leg folded.

Yun rolled, scattered poison powder and expected him to withdraw.

He did.

But the route officers did not enter the cloud.

They activated fans from outside it.

The powder lifted.

Dispersed.

The cloud vanished around her.

Yun stared at them.

“You rehearsed this.”

Shunto answered from beyond the clearing air.

“Yes.”

At least he was honest.

Yun attacked him directly.

For three months she had avoided that whenever possible.

Now the only path out was through the man who had built the cage.

She moved low.

Two needles.

One false.

A hidden third from the wrist.

Shunto yielded to the first two.

The third nearly reached his neck.

Nearly.

He caught her forearm with cloth wrapped around his own glove, twisted once and released before she could transfer anything.

Her hand opened involuntarily.

The needle fell.

Fourth contact.

Fifth.

Elbow.

Shoulder.

Hip.

Never long.

Never skin.

Each one turned off a little more movement.

Yun changed poison families.

Shunto did not give her enough exposure.

She changed timing.

He changed rhythm.

She poisoned the ground.

Route officers neutralized it.

She targeted his left-arm reroute.

It was gone.

Not healed.

Rebuilt.

He had redesigned the circulation response that she used in Chapter 328’s fight and denied her the same lesson twice.

Yun understood then.

Not that she had lost.

She had known that was possible from the beginning.

She understood why.

Shunto had spent three months letting every escape write his final capture plan.

Her successes were inside it.

Her poison to the arm.

Her walls.

Her smoke.

Her preference for environmental contamination.

Her refusal to endanger civilians.

Her route logic.

Her need to leave once the evidence became sufficient.

He had not waited for Yun to become foolish.

He had become prepared for Yun while she remained herself.

That was worse.

It was also, infuriatingly, fair.

Shunto caught the back of her collar through cloth.

Yun drove a blade backward.

He released.

A route officer’s staff struck the blade from her hand without entering arm’s reach.

Yun reached for the notebook case.

Shunto saw it.

His next pulse hit the shoulder line controlling that arm.

Her fingers stopped two inches short.

No.

Yun forced circulation through an alternate path.

Pain flashed white across her chest.

Her hand moved one inch.

Shunto’s eyes widened slightly.

Then his hook caught the diagnostic case strap.

Yun cut it herself.

The case dropped between them.

She kicked it under the collapsed wall.

One officer moved.

Shunto said, “Leave it.”

The officer stopped.

Yun looked at him.

Interesting.

He wanted her first.

The material second.

That gave her one last option.

She bit down on a capsule hidden behind her molar.

Shunto moved faster than before.

Not to her mouth.

His thumb pressed a point below her jaw through the glove.

Her bite reflex released.

The capsule remained intact.

Yun stared at him.

Shunto’s expression was exhausted.

Not triumphant.

“Enough.”

She hated that word now.

Yun drove her forehead toward his nose.

He shifted.

Her forehead struck his cheek instead.

A satisfying amount of pain appeared in his eyes.

Good.

Then he completed the lock.

Shoulder.

Elbow.

Hip.

Neck line.

Circulation folded into controlled channels that kept her conscious while denying the explosive reroutes she used for escape.

Her knees hit the wet road.

Shunto remained behind her, avoiding every known poison contact point.

Yun tried one final alternate pulse.

Nothing.

Not because she had no qi.

Because every route she preferred had been anticipated.

She breathed once.

Twice.

The world stayed upright.

Shunto said, “Captured alive.”

Yun laughed weakly.

“You sound pleased.”

“I sound tired.”

“That too.”

He looked toward the diagnostic case beneath the wall.

Then at her wrist brace.

He did not yet know which mattered more.

Good.

That meant the information war was still alive.

Yun lifted her head as far as the lock allowed.

Three months.

Shunto had finally won the immediate contest.

He had her alive.

He had the road.

He had the room she was about to enter.

But he did not have Luo’s mind.

He did not have everything she had memorized.

And somewhere beyond Shinrin, one coded packet might already be moving toward Wuyue.

Yun closed her eyes.

The chase was over.

The warning was not.
